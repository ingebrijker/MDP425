# Intervention Recommendations

The following recommendations are based on the findings of this study and provide guidance for the planning, design, construction, and monitoring of future coastal interventions. They aim to improve the effectiveness and long-term sustainability of interventions designed to support mangrove restoration and reduce coastal erosion.

### 1. Understand the Integral Sediment System Before Building

Coastal interventions should only be designed after the wider sediment system is understood, by examining erosion and accretion hotspots and human influences. As an intervention captures sediment locally, it could lead to erosion at another site. The design of structures is recommended to be preceded by a coastal-scale sediment analysis, for which shoreline analysis with satellite imagery can serve as a starting point. It is also important to examine whether sufficient sediment is available within the water.

### 2. Conditions for an Effective Breakwater

Most of the following points are associations and not proven, as the factors could not be separated from each other. The best-performing breakwaters are located in regions where there is already a high sediment availability and where waves are the primary reason for erosion. Long, continuous intervention segments placed around 100–150 m from the mangrove edge create the most stable accretion patterns behind them, and older interventions (10+ years) show more positive effects than newer ones. Thus, interventions can slow the retreat locally, but they do not remove its source, as the dominant causes of retreat are often regional, such as a declining sediment supply or subsidence.

### 3. Allow More Time Between Construction and Planting

Often, there is only a short period between the construction of the breakwater and the planting of mangroves, during which the conditions have only changed slightly. By waiting for the construction to adapt to the environment and for sedimentation to take place, the location becomes more suitable for mangrove establishment. The waiting period should not be fixed in advance, but linked to monitoring of the accretion rate, the grain size distribution of the upper soil layer, and the bed level relative to the inundation range suitable for the selected mangrove species.

### 4. Combine Hard and Soft Measures Until a Sufficient Mangrove Belt Has Developed

Neither mangroves nor grey structures alone appear to be sufficient. We therefore recommend a hybrid approach in which breakwaters provide wave attenuation and sediment trapping while the mangrove belt develops. The structures should be maintained until the mangrove belt is more than 500 m wide. Then, it can take over the wave-attenuating function. Repeatedly relocating breakwaters is neither sustainable nor feasible. An alternative could be to use bamboo fences in areas where wave heights are significantly lower, as they are temporary, easier to move, less expensive, and might be more adaptive to subsidence.

### 5. Repeat the Study and Continue Monitoring

The breakwaters were built in 2020 or later, so their effect cannot yet be judged in its entirety. Therefore, the study should be repeated after a few years. Furthermore, design factors such as distance to the mangrove edge, continuity, and type of breakwater should be tested separately in a pilot study and compared with a similar stretch of coastline without any intervention. In addition, waves should be measured in front of and behind the structure, in combination with bed-level surveys and suspended-sediment research. The Living Lab should continue this monitoring to keep track of long-term performance.
